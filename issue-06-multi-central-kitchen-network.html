Costara Insights • Issue 06 • Multi-Kitchen

Do Not Run Four Kitchens. Run One Production Network.

Optimize total cost across capacity, production, logistics and service.

Multiple kitchens should not automatically duplicate each other

When every kitchen produces the same full menu, the operation may duplicate equipment, manpower, stock, cleaning, supervision and utility demand. Duplication may be necessary for resilience, but it should be intentional rather than inherited.

Start with demand and capacity

Map meals by location, service time and menu requirement. Then measure each kitchen’s usable capacity, equipment constraints, labour capability, storage and delivery reach. Production should be allocated to the kitchen that can safely and reliably deliver the required output at the lowest total cost.

Balance profit, not workload

Equal meal allocation may look fair while being economically inefficient. One kitchen may have spare capacity and low incremental cost, while another becomes expensive above a certain volume because of extra batches, overtime or transport. The question is where the next 1,000 meals should be produced and delivered.

Specialization can create efficiency

Where food safety, transport time and service requirements allow, kitchens may specialize in high-volume starches, proteins, special diets, sauces, bakery or assembly rather than duplicating all processes. Specialization must be tested against logistics, contingency capacity and quality risk.

Costara approach

Treat all kitchens as one production network. Compare production cost, energy cost, labour, equipment utilisation, logistics and cost per delivered meal before changing allocation. Preserve contingency capacity so efficiency does not remove operational resilience.
